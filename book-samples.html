Introduction
The Choice You Keep Making

This book is about marriage—and the good, the bad, and the ugly that come with it. All of it is real. You’ll have good days that turn into epic days, and epic days that slam straight into disaster. That constant change and uncertainty that runs through the rest of life runs through marriage too—without exception.

So why do some couples look like they have it all together while others feel like a constant battle with very little happiness? The answer isn’t hard to understand; it’s just hard to apply. The couples who look happy may not be. And the couples who are working on their marriage all the time might be the happiest they’ve ever been. Looking at other people’s relationships is a waste of precious energy. It’s like watching someone else run on a treadmill and expecting your body to burn the calories. We all do it—at work, with money, with status—and then we drag the same habit into marriage: What are they doing right that I’m doing wrong?

Here’s the truth I’ve learned again and again in counseling: at the foundation, our struggles in marriage are strikingly similar. The details differ, the specifics change, but the core issues are familiar to every couple. This book is here to offer practical solutions that apply differently to every couple, because every couple is different. My clients often hear me say, the details don’t matter (yet), it’s the foundations that matter.

Foundations Before Details

If you want to understand what’s really going on, you have to think foundationally. That means resisting the urge to obsess over the details that soak up attention and fuel arguments—the little habits that drive you crazy, the easy jabs you know you’ll regret later, the mental spreadsheets of everything your spouse “always” or “never” does. The topics that may be ok for others but you and your spouse fight about. The details that hurt. The details that nobody knows about you but your spouse. The details that invite all sorts of emotions to the forefront of any potential confrontation. Those details are seductive because they shine a spotlight in that moment on the other person. That spotlight is ok as long as it is anywhere but on us. They keep us from looking inward at the places we know, deep down, need refining.

Change is difficult. Admitting that a part of you—one that’s lived for years without much accountability—needs to grow? That’s tough for all of us. Contrary to popular belief that is not an admission of guilt or wrong doing, in fact I call that emotional intelligence. Being self aware at mindset, attitude, words, and emotions you bring to the table, as well as a knowledge of the potential changes to the environment that they produce, and to do this willingly… is a skill that takes time to develop.

Society is also a massive contributor to paying attention to emotional and relational details prematurely. We are so aggressively aware of how we feel, and validate and rationalize how we have the right to feel that way, we forget that in order to communicate that effectively, we must first know HOW to communicate those feelings. This is a skill that takes practice within the confines of marriage. In order to produce the ability to get well versed on the WHAT we feel and WHY we feel that way, we must work with our spouse to find out HOW to do that well. Details encourage us to focus on the what and the why… Foundations give us confidence on the how to do that better.

A Lesson from the Counseling Room

In this book I will talk about real couples (their identity will stay anonymous) that I have encountered in private practice that will highlight the practicality of the principals in this book as we go forward. However, before I ever sat with married couples, I worked with teens in the foster care system preparing to launch into adulthood. Over and over I watched the same pattern: even when life practically shouted, Something has to change, many young people dug in their heels, insisting the environment needed to change first. Sooner or later, reality smacked them in the face and forced a choice—grow or fail. For most, that lesson didn’t sink in overnight; it took months or years and more than a few hard knocks.

Then, working with couples after hours, I saw the exact same mindset. Two adults, looking at each other across a couch, both convinced that the problem would be solved if the other person changed first. That “their truth” was “the truth”. The common belief? For things to get better—life, marriage, “us”—the environment must change, not me.

So I started asking a simple question: Why? Why, in the middle of strain, knowing that a shift in attitude, tone, or routine on our part would help, do we keep waiting for the other person to move first? Can we really demand change from someone else through criticism, confrontation, or withdrawal if we’re unwilling to do what we’re asking of them?

At the foundation of almost every marital conflict, you’ll find two people staring at each other like mirrors—each waiting for the reflection to move first.

When each spouse becomes willing to change themselves for the sake of the other, a marriage doesn’t just survive—it flourishes. If you want to say “I do” with confidence every day, you have to be committed to the willingness to change every day.

Autoplastic vs. Alloplastic: The Mindset That Shapes Marriage

Clinically, there are two helpful words here. Autoplastic means I’m willing to change and adapt to my environment when there’s resistance. Alloplastic means I try to change my environment (or everyone in it) rather than change myself.

Apply those mindsets to marriage and you’ll see the pattern. Couples who flourish are usually two autoplastic people: What can I do for us? How can I love you better today? They bring a daily willingness to adapt, serve, be uncomfortable, be vulnerable, and—even when necessary—be wrong. That doesn’t create confusion; it creates resolution. When each person is thinking, What can I do for you? instead of What are you not doing for me?—peace has a place to grow.

Now picture two alloplastic partners: each waiting, each insisting, each frustrated. That’s where conflict calcifies. When an immovable object meets an unstoppable force, nothing good happens. The details of the argument are almost irrelevant; the standoff is the problem.

Many marriages struggle when one spouse has a willing spirit and the other doesn’t. Maybe they’re still together for outside reasons—kids, finances, fear of what friends will think. The unwilling partner often ends up getting their way; the willing partner keeps bending to keep the peace. Over time—this timeline varies but it’s predictable—the person who’s always accommodating runs out of steam. Meanwhile, the less-willing spouse may think everything’s fine, unaware of how constant giving without return erodes a person’s hope.

That instability can ferment into depression, infidelity, or a quiet decision to leave. These are the couples I see most. One of my earliest observations came while sitting in on a marriage session: the wife did most of the talking, explaining how her husband “wasn’t himself.” She described the earlier years—he came home from work, made dinner, handled the kids, even did the laundry. “Superman,” she said. When I asked him what he did for fun, it was like a cannon went off.

“I have no hobbies. I have nothing I consider fun. I go to work. I come home to a messy house. I make dinner. I do the laundry and the shopping… When would I have time for a hobby?”

She stared at him like she didn’t recognize the man beside her. She hadn’t realized the price his unreturned effort was charging. He wasn’t checked out; he was hurt and exhausted.

Early on I learned another truism: no two people are the same, so no two people agree 100% of the time. If a couple never disagrees, someone’s not being honest. That’s why daily self-evaluation matters. Ask: Am I willing to be who my spouse needs today? Am I aware of their needs? Can I do anything about them right now? If the wife in that story had asked herself those questions, they might never have needed counseling. And to be clear—I’m not picking on wives. I’ve seen this dynamic play out with roles reversed just as often. This isn’t a gender thing; it’s a human thing. It’s learned—and it can be unlearned.

Which mindset do you lean toward—willing to change, or waiting for the other to change? Think back to a hard season. Was your spouse (and the marriage you share) enough motivation to try something different? Is the hope of a peaceful and happy marriage enough to nudge you toward growth?

Remember: every marriage is unique. What you’re changing into—or out of—will look different than anyone else’s path because your marriage is different than anyone else’s. Don’t focus on the details until you commit to this: I am willing to change for the good of our relationship and the environment that we create.

And one more thing—it’s never a question of Do I need to change? The answer is yes. The only question that really matters day after day—the one that keeps you saying I do long after the wedding—is: Am I willing to change today?

Chapter One
Understanding the Vows

Saying I Do Every Day means learning to understand the vows you spoke. Everyone says vows on that special day in some way, shape, or form. Some are formal, some are poetic, and a few are sung. I’ve heard some that stopped a room and others that made the crowd laugh—but nearly every couple, no matter how they phrase it, makes a version of the same promise.

A vow is not filler; it’s a promise—a commitment to uphold an action, to embody a posture, and in marriage, to become part of something larger than yourself.

Do you take this person, to have and to hold, to love and to cherish, for better or worse, for richer or poorer, in sickness and in health, to love and to cherish, from this day forward, till death do you part?

Those words are spoken on what most people call the happiest day of their lives. No one walks down the aisle thinking, I can’t wait to divorce this person in a few years. And yet that’s where so many marriages end up. The divorce rate in the United States still hovers just above fifty percent. More than half the couples who make these same promises before God, family, and friends eventually find themselves unable—or unwilling—to keep them.

So what happens between the wedding and the divorce?

For most couples I’ve worked with, it begins quietly—with small sentences like They’ve changed or I’m just not happy anymore. They sound simple, but they signal a slow drift: a journey made of straws added one at a time to the camel’s back, or baby steps that quietly move two people in opposite directions.

A counselor I respect once told me, “Relationships are always moving. They never stay still.” That’s true. Every relationship is either moving closer together or further apart—never the same. Think about your best friend from high school. Do you still talk regularly? How about the kids from your old neighborhood or the coworkers from that job you left years ago? Time and seasons shift, and if we aren’t intentional, connection fades.

Marriage is no different. Couples drift apart not because they stop loving each other but because they stop being intentional about staying connected. They forget about the vows. Days turn into weeks, weeks into months, months into years, and one day they look across the table and barely recognize the person sitting there. The distance feels impossible to close—but it’s not. It only becomes impossible when we refuse to change course.

My wife and I learned early in our marriage that staying connected requires work. We do what we call mental-health check-ins. It’s simple: we ask each other, How are you, really? Some weeks one of us is thriving and the other is exhausted. When that happens, the stronger one rows the metaphorical boat for a while. Other weeks, we’re both tired and have to row together slowly. The key is that the conversation happens—we stay aware of each other’s state.

That, to me, is the essence of the vows. It’s not one person doing all the work while the other coasts; it’s two people carrying the load together, taking turns when needed. Communication keeps us balanced.

Open dialogue—like asking the other to “take the wheel for a day”—has saved our marriage more than once. It’s not glamorous, but it’s powerful. It reminds us that our marriage isn’t me versus you; it’s us versus the world.

When couples remember that, they rediscover the person they promised to love. The partner who felt distant isn’t gone; they’re just waiting to be seen again. Peace and happiness start to return the moment both people decide the vows still matter.

Sometimes we wake up and decide, This is going to be a bad day. Before we even get out of bed, we let a feeling make a decision for us, and then we spend the rest of the day collecting evidence to prove ourselves right. We all do it—it’s human nature. Feelings set the verdict, and our minds start building the case.

We do the same thing in marriage. We collect evidence to justify distance: See, they didn’t text me first. They forgot to say thank you. They never notice my effort anymore.

It’s self-fulfilling. When you look for proof that the marriage is falling apart, you’ll find it. When you look for proof that it’s worth fighting for, you’ll find that too.

In counseling, I use an exercise to make this visible. I give each spouse a sheet of paper with two columns. One side reads Things I Do for My Spouse; the other, Things My Spouse Does for Me. They fill them out separately. The couples who are struggling most usually hand in papers where the first column is full and the second is maybe half that size—or empty.

Then we swap them. Each person reads the other’s list. Without fail, they’re shocked. Both have been giving more than the other realized. The issue was never a lack of effort; it was a lack of recognition. Awareness begins the comeback journey.

Effort being made requires effort being recognized.

A study once explored why people quit their jobs, and the results were staggering: nearly eighty percent said it wasn’t the workload or the pay—it was a lack of recognition. They left stability, comfort, even security because they didn’t feel seen.

If being unrecognized at work can push people to walk away from a paycheck, imagine what being unseen in marriage can do to a heart.

Recognition is not flattery; it’s acknowledgment. It tells your spouse, I see what you’re trying to do, and it matters. When both people start seeing again—really seeing—the same relationship begins to feel brand new.

Effort being made and recognized may require change in the specifics of what we are actually doing, and change is hard for everyone. If it weren’t, we’d all be in superhero shape with perfect habits and unbreakable discipline. The truth is, we set resolutions, we get motivated, and then we start negotiating with ourselves. When the heart decides to improve, the mind starts calculating excuses: I’ll start Monday. I’ll wait until after vacation. Let me get through this busy season first. That internal tug-of-war—what I call reasoning—shows up in marriage too.

In counseling, I see it all the time: the same mental math that keeps people from going to the gym keeps couples from having that overdue conversation or reaching out first after an argument. We convince ourselves that the other person should make the first move. Why should I be intentional when they’re not? Why should I put in effort when I’m the only one trying?

It’s easy to find logic that supports passivity because change always asks something uncomfortable of us. But the reality is simple: relationships are living things. They’re always moving—closer or further apart—and “staying still” isn’t an option. If we aren’t intentional about moving toward one another, we drift away by default.

A couple with no shared intention ends up blaming each other, and those complaints often sound perfectly reasonable out loud. Why should I go out of my way when they won’t? Why invest energy in someone who doesn’t invest back? On paper, those are valid questions. But in practice, they’re often self-protection and fear dressed up as logic.

From a practical standpoint, leading by example doesn’t always make sense. Relationships aren’t equations—they’re built on humility, and humility almost always moves things forward.

Relationships don’t thrive on logic; they thrive on love expressed in the language your spouse understands. Choosing to act in love—especially when it’s inconvenient—isn’t weak. It’s courageous. The vows you spoke were never about convenience. They were about commitment, and commitment is most visible when it costs something.

There’s a line from the apostle Paul that captures the tension perfectly: “What I want to do, I don’t do; and what I don’t want to do, I do.” It’s human nature to know the right thing and still resist it. If you look for a reason to avoid being intentional in your marriage, you’ll find one every time.

Now, before we go further, let me be clear—this doesn’t mean ignoring wisdom or staying in harm’s way. If you’re in an abusive or toxic relationship, please seek help and leave safely. But for the majority of marriages that end under the phrase irreconcilable differences, the root cause isn’t danger—it’s an unwillingness by one or both partners to change for the sake of the relationship.

Every couple faces conflict: money, intimacy, stress, family, unmet expectations. If you’re hunting for reasons to quit, you’ll find them. But if you choose to search for reasons to stay, you’ll find those too.

Because relationships are always in motion, there must always be deliberate actions pulling them back together. When no one moves, human nature and emotional gravity take over, and distance wins. Drift isn’t loud or dramatic—it’s subtle. It’s the choice of comfort over connection, one quiet day at a time.

It takes work to stay close. My wife and I know this well. We’ve both worked in high-stress professions caring for the mental and physical health of others. Some weeks I’m energized while she’s drained. On those weeks I’ll tell her, “Let me row the boat for a while.” Other times she takes the oars and tells me to rest. And on our best weeks, we row together.

Those check-ins aren’t glamorous, but they’re sacred. They remind us that our marriage isn’t me versus you—it’s us versus everything else. That, to me, is what the vows really mean.

I tell every couple in my private practice the same thing: look at the person beside you and decide that you have their back completely. Serve when it’s inconvenient. Encourage when they least deserve it. Learn how they give and receive love, and then speak that language fluently. Ask questions. Be curious. And then—trust that they’re trying to do the same for you.

When both partners commit to daily intentionality, something remarkable happens. The person you fell in love with years ago begins to re-emerge. They were never gone—just buried under fatigue, distraction, and unspoken expectations. Peace and happiness within the relationship aren’t miracles; they’re momentum. Start small, keep rowing, and eventually the tide changes.

Modern culture has complicated this simplicity. We’ve flipped the emphasis from what is important to what is exciting. We prepare endlessly for a wedding but rarely for a marriage. Couples will spend twelve months choosing colors, playlists, photographers, and venues—but maybe two hours seriously discussing communication, conflict, shared expectations, or what love and respect mean to each of them.

The wedding is a day; the marriage is a lifetime. The vows aren’t decoration for a ceremony—they’re the instructions for what comes next.

When I first moved to the South, I was stunned by how elaborate wedding celebrations could be—multiple showers, engagement parties, entire weekends built around photos and events. The celebration is beautiful, but the purpose can get lost. Too often, all the energy goes into the production and almost none into preparation. The result is a marriage that begins with glamour but no grounding.

It isn’t that celebration is wrong—it’s that celebration without preparation leaves a foundation of confetti and little else. When the noise fades, couples often find themselves standing in the quiet with no plan for what comes next.

I tell young couples this often: you’re not preparing for a perfect day; you’re preparing for a shared, imperfect life.

I am reminded of a couple… we will call them Couple A. Couple A came in about 18 months into their marriage on the verge of divorce. Both of them young, in their mid-twenties, but on the verge of separation due to the reality that I can only describe as expectation vs. reality. This couple came in for several months focused on their own point of view, having a difficult time trying to understand one another’s point of view. I had asked them in the session if they had received any pre-marital counseling and both responded as if to say “I wish.” They explained that it was not that they were against the idea; it was that they were so busy with the planning of the wedding, scheduling the wedding, and celebrating the engagement it was just not something that they felt they would have time to do. So… they did not go. I asked if there were any requirements from their minister and they stated that there was not. They seemed to agree that they vaguely were discussing that with her parents, but not really encouraged to follow through. Everything worked out in counseling and they are still together doing well, but both admit that doing the work 18 months in was way more difficult than making that a part of the preparation. I had described what they did as trying to storm proof a house in the middle of a storm, rather than when the weather was nice and calm, to which they agreed wholeheartedly.

The vows are more than tradition; they’re anchors. They were written to hold us steady through the storms they name. For better or for worse, for richer or for poorer, in sickness and in health—each phrase admits that adversity will come. The promise isn’t to avoid those seasons but to endure them together.

When couples recite those lines, they’re saying: I’ve seen enough of life to know it won’t always be easy, and I still choose you. That daily, intentional choice is the heartbeat of marriage.

Somewhere between the altar and everyday life, we forget that vows are verbs. They aren’t one-time statements; they’re daily actions. They need renewal each morning. Without that renewal, we treat them like history—something we did rather than something we do.

Human beings will go to extraordinary lengths to avoid discomfort. We’re wired that way. It’s easier to postpone the doctor’s appointment than to face the diagnosis. It’s easier to stay busy than to admit we’re lonely. We rationalize, distract, or blame to sidestep pain instead of learning from it. But emotional maturity isn’t about avoiding pain; it’s about facing it with the right perspective.

In marriage, avoidance often takes on a quieter form. When conflict arises, instead of addressing the issue together, couples start protecting themselves from each other. The result isn’t peace—it’s distance. You can live under the same roof, eat at the same table, and still be miles apart emotionally.

The vows exist to keep us grounded in those moments. They remind us that discomfort is part of the deal. They don’t promise happiness; they promise presence. They aren’t a guarantee that life will be easy; they’re a declaration that you’ll show up when it’s not. Sickness, scarcity, and struggle aren’t exceptions to the promise—they’re the very reason it exists.

When a couple embraces that truth, hardship stops being an interruption to marriage and becomes part of its curriculum. Challenges stop being the things that threaten your relationship and start becoming the things that strengthen it. The pain isn’t wasted when it’s faced together.

That’s the shift healthy couples make. They don’t avoid tension; they learn how to handle it without breaking the bond. They lean in with humility, curiosity, and faith. They start to see conflict not as a sign that something’s wrong, but as an opportunity to learn something new about themselves and about each other.

I once worked with a couple who’d been together for nearly twenty years. They were kind, committed, and deeply tired. When they sat down on my couch, it was clear they still cared for each other but had become experts in avoidance. Every conversation ended with silence because it was easier than risking another argument. Over time, that silence turned into distance.

They came to therapy convinced that communication was their main problem. But the real issue wasn’t their lack of words—it was their lack of safety. They were so afraid of saying the wrong thing that they stopped saying anything at all. Once they learned to see discomfort not as danger but as an invitation to honesty, the walls began to fall. It wasn’t fast or easy, but they found their way back—not by avoiding pain, but by facing it together.

That’s what growth looks like in marriage. It’s not loud or dramatic; it’s quiet courage repeated over and over again.

The truth is, every marriage will face a choice between avoiding pain and pursuing growth. Avoidance feels easier in the moment, but it always costs more in the long run. Growth feels harder at first, but it leads to peace that lasts.

Couples who choose growth understand that the vows they made weren’t about avoiding storms; they were about holding hands through them. They discover that faithfulness isn’t found in perfection—it’s found in consistency.

Many people believe that leaving a difficult marriage for a new one will solve their problems. What they often discover, painfully, is that they’ve brought themselves along. Habits, defenses, and fears travel well. The scenery changes, but the patterns don’t. That’s why second and third marriages statistically end at even higher rates than first ones. The math is simple: you can’t outrun yourself.

The goal isn’t to find a life without friction; it’s to develop the skills to face friction faithfully. That’s what the vows were designed for—not to prevent hardship, but to guide us through it.

Couples who stay connected aren’t lucky; they’re learners. They practice the rhythm of reconnecting. They understand that the vows aren’t just romantic poetry from centuries past—they’re a modern blueprint for how to love well.

To have and to hold means choosing presence. For better or for worse means keeping perspective. For richer or for poorer means staying united in partnership. In sickness and in health means showing compassion. To love and to cherish means choosing tenderness. Till death do us part means choosing commitment every single day.

Understanding the vows is understanding the framework for marriage itself. They’re not old-fashioned ideals; they’re timeless anchors. Every healthy couple I’ve ever worked with, without realizing it, ends up circling back to those same principles. They call us to humility, sacrifice, and faithfulness.

Marriage will always move in the direction of your daily decisions. Every morning, you get to choose again how seriously you’ll take the promise you made. It doesn’t matter what yesterday looked like; today gives you another chance to live it better. When both people practice that awareness, even small gestures—listening fully, offering forgiveness, showing gratitude—become sacred ground.

Ordinary moments become the proving ground for extraordinary love. The vows you spoke years ago are still waiting to be lived today. They’re not fragile; they’re flexible. They bend with you through every season of change, and if you keep returning to them, they’ll hold.

That’s what it means to say I do every day—to keep choosing each other, even when it’s hard, even when it’s quiet, even when it doesn’t make sense to anyone else.

Everyday “I Do” Action

Write down your vows exactly as they were spoken—or, if you no longer have them, write new ones that reflect where you are now.

Read them aloud together. Notice which lines feel easy and which ones feel heavy.

Pick one phrase and ask each other, What would it look like to live this out this week?

At the end of the week, talk about what changed.

You’ll be surprised how small moments of intention can reignite connection. Vows aren’t words to be remembered; they’re promises to be repeated through action.

Chapter 1 Summary: Understanding the Vows

Marriage isn’t sustained by feelings—it’s built on a series of intentional choices repeated every day. The vows we spoke weren’t ceremonial words; they were a roadmap for how to navigate the seasons ahead. Understanding those vows means seeing them not as promises to avoid difficulty, but as commitments to face it together. Each line—to have and to hold, for better or worse, in sickness and in health—reminds us that love is a verb, not a memory. When couples reconnect with the meaning behind their vows, they rediscover that peace and joy are not random—they’re built.

Chapter Three
For Better or For Worse

Marriage is not a single moment—it’s a series of choices that never stop asking something of you. Every vow sounds simple when spoken in front of friends and family: for better or for worse. But what happens when the “worse” finally shows up? What does commitment look like when the honeymoon glow fades and the real work begins?

This chapter is hard to write because the word commitment has become a moving target. For some, it means loyalty until things get uncomfortable. For others, it’s a lifetime promise, even when comfort disappears. By definition, commitment means “the state or quality of being dedicated to a cause, activity, or person.” It’s a word that should feel weighty, but somewhere along the way, it’s lost its gravity.

When I searched the word “commitment” online for this book, the first example sentence that popped up read: “Young people delay major commitments, including marriage and children.” That line stopped me cold—not because it’s wrong, but because it’s revealing. Commitment has become something to avoid or postpone, not something to be proud of. Many people today approach it like a lease instead of a covenant. They’ll renew it if it’s still convenient.

And yet, deep down, we all crave the security of knowing someone is all in. We long for a love that lasts, but we’ve grown afraid of what it costs to keep it.

The Disappearing Art of Commitment

The truth is, healthy marriages don’t disappear—they just don’t get much attention. For every one solid, joyful, lifelong union, there’s another that collapses under the weight of unmet expectations and misunderstood communication. We are teaching the next generation—directly or indirectly—that marriage is optional, disposable, or simply too hard to maintain. Even in the church, the divorce rate mirrors that of the culture around it.

Each failed marriage leaves behind not just two hurt people, but a ripple effect—a blueprint for what the next generation believes is possible. Children raised watching chaos or silence between their parents often build their own relationships with those same blueprints. Some will use them as “what not to do” lessons, but many others will unconsciously repeat what they’ve seen.

It’s not hopeless—it’s human. We imitate what we know until someone teaches us differently. That’s why it’s so important to show what real commitment looks like.

Commitment is not about feeling in love every day. It’s about choosing to act in love even when the feelings don’t cooperate. It’s a vow to stay in the boat when the water gets rough—because leaving the boat only ensures you’ll drown alone.

Our culture has become obsessed with moments and allergic to maintenance. We celebrate beginnings with confetti and hashtags but rarely talk about the work that comes after the pictures fade. We know how to plan a perfect day, but not always how to sustain an imperfect life.

Marriage, however, isn’t a single moment—it’s the collection of every moment that follows. The couples who last aren’t the ones who threw the biggest wedding or posted the prettiest photos. They’re the ones who kept showing up when the music stopped and the dishes still needed washing.

I tell couples in premarital sessions all the time: You’re not preparing for a wedding; you’re preparing for a lifetime of decisions that will test the vows you make that day. The world rewards spectacle, but marriage rewards steadiness. The quiet decision to stay, to forgive, to rebuild, and to try again—those are the choices that make love durable.

We don’t need more cinematic proposals or destination weddings. We need more Tuesday-night grace, more long conversations after misunderstandings, more people choosing each other even when it isn’t convenient.

I mentioned this before in Chapter 1… but as a young adult pastor, I watched many couples—people who had spent a year planning the wedding—walk into my office just a few years later, struggling to communicate or stay connected. I began to notice a pattern: the couples who poured their energy into the wedding day often neglected to prepare for the married life that followed.

It wasn’t intentional; it was cultural. We’ve romanticized the beginning so much that we’ve forgotten to prepare for the middle.

I remember one couple who told me, long after their divorce, that they wished someone had forced them to slow down before saying “I do.” They were deeply in love but had never discussed how to handle conflict, finances, or even where they wanted to live. They thought marriage would “work itself out.” Instead, it worked them over.

Their story is more common than you’d think. Love by itself isn’t enough to sustain a marriage—it needs tools, time, and truth.

Learning to Communicate in the “Worse” Seasons

My wife and I have a saying that grew out of one of those “worse” moments: “I love you always, but I don’t like you right now.”

The first time she said it, I laughed—mostly because it was true. Over the years, that line became a way to hit pause before emotions made a bad situation worse. It gave us permission to feel what we were feeling without rewriting the foundation of our relationship.

We’re wired differently, and that’s okay. I like to resolve tension right away; she prefers to take a breath and revisit things when emotions cool down. Early in our marriage, those differences made conflict hard. I saw her need for space as avoidance. She saw my urgency as pressure.

Eventually, we learned to meet in the middle. Now we ask each other, “I know you love me, but do you still like me right now?” Sometimes the answer is yes. Sometimes it’s no. But it’s honest, and honesty always brings perspective.

We often remind couples who fear that needing space means falling out of love: it doesn’t. Think of it like parenting—you love your kids unconditionally, but that doesn’t mean you always like them. Love that lasts allows for honesty without fear of rejection.

The key is remembering that marriage is bigger than our momentary feelings. It’s bigger than moods, preferences, or who’s right this time. It’s about staying rooted in the belief that even when things feel broken, the commitment still holds.

The phrase “for better or for worse” was never meant to be poetic filler—it’s a survival strategy. It means I will not measure this relationship by its comfort level, but by its purpose.

A wise mentor once told me, “Anyone will sit down with you to eat steak. But when all you’ve got left is beans, you’ll see who’s really with you.”

That’s what “for better or for worse” looks like in real life. Marriage isn’t about escaping storms; it’s about learning how to share the umbrella when the rain comes down.

The Elbow and the Punch

Intent is one of the most misunderstood and underestimated dynamics in any marriage. It hides underneath every conversation, every disagreement, and every misunderstanding. You can’t see intent—you can only feel its effects. And that’s exactly where most couples run into trouble.

We often assume we know what our spouse meant by something. “I know what that look meant.” “I know what she was really saying.” “I could tell by his tone exactly what he meant.”

But the truth is, what we think we’re reading is rarely intention—it’s emotion, filtered through our own experience. We interpret through our mood, our past, and our pain. And when pain is the lens, everything looks like a threat.

When we feel hurt, our brains don’t slow down to analyze motives. They jump to self-protection: I’m under attack—defend yourself. That’s the moment where communication breaks down. The issue isn’t what was said or done—it’s what we decided it meant.

Proverbs 16:2 says, “All a person’s ways seem pure to them, but motives are weighed by the Lord.” In other words, even when we believe our motives are good, only God truly sees them for what they are. So how much harder must it be for two imperfect humans, in the heat of emotion, to accurately judge one another’s intentions?

We can’t read each other’s hearts perfectly. But we can choose to trust them, especially when we feel the opposite urge.

The Story of the Elbow and the Punch

Back when I lived in Michigan, winter meant long, freezing months spent indoors. My friends and I would find the nearest gym and play basketball to burn off energy. Basketball isn’t a gentle game—it’s fast, physical, and full of collisions. Every now and then, you catch someone with an elbow while pivoting or going up for a rebound. It hurts—sometimes you end up with a busted lip or a sore jaw—but everyone on that court knows the difference between an accident and an attack.

The next day, you’re back on the court with the same guys, no grudges, because the pain wasn’t personal—it was an elbow.

That’s the difference between an elbow and a punch. An elbow hurts, but it wasn’t meant to. A punch hurts because it was meant to. The difference between the two isn’t the pain—it’s the intent behind the impact.

In marriage, elbows happen all the time. You say something careless after a long day, and your spouse hears rejection instead of exhaustion. You try to solve a problem that they only wanted empathy for. You make a joke to ease tension, and it lands as disrespect.

You didn’t mean to hurt them—it was an elbow—but they felt a punch.

And here’s where things unravel: when one spouse treats every elbow like a punch, both start swinging. You defend, they retaliate, and before you know it, what started as a misunderstanding turns into a full-blown argument where no one remembers how it began.

When Love Misfires

I once counseled a couple—let’s call them Couple C. The husband in Couple C was analytical and solution-oriented. Whenever the wife came to him upset, he jumped straight into problem-solving mode. To him, this was love—helping fix what hurt her.

But to his wife, it felt dismissive. She didn’t want a fix; she wanted comfort. When he offered logic, she heard distance. When she got quiet, he heard rejection.

Both were convinced the other had stopped caring.

In our session, I asked the husband, “When you try to fix her problem, what’s your intention?” He said, “I just want to help her. I hate seeing her upset.”

Then I asked the wife, “When he does that, what do you assume his intention is?” She replied, “That he doesn’t care enough to listen.”

Same moment. Two entirely different interpretations.

That’s the elbow and the punch in real time. He meant to help—she felt dismissed. Both were technically right, but the emotional math didn’t add up.

Once they learned to identify the difference—to name the elbow before it became a punch—everything changed. The husband started asking, “Do you want me to listen or help?” and the wife began recognizing his fixing as care, not coldness.

It didn’t make their marriage perfect, but it made it clear.

How to Tell the Difference

So how do you know whether what just happened was an elbow or a punch? It’s not a perfect science, but these questions can help:

Pattern: Is this behavior typical of your spouse, or out of character? Elbows are accidental and occasional. Punches are repetitive and deliberate.

Presence: Do they seem aware of the pain they caused? Someone who threw an elbow usually feels bad. A puncher doesn’t care.

Posture: Do they lean in to make it right, or pull away to defend themselves? People with good intentions reach for you, not away from you.

If you pause long enough to ask these questions before reacting, you’ll often realize your spouse didn’t mean to hurt you—they just mis-stepped.

That pause—the moment between pain and reaction—is where maturity lives.

And if you get it wrong and react too fast? Grace is the bridge back. You can still say, “That hurt,” without accusing, “You meant to hurt me.” You can still draw boundaries without building walls.

Healthy couples don’t avoid elbows; they recover from them faster.

Trust doesn’t mean pretending the elbow didn’t hurt—it means believing your partner didn’t mean to throw it.

When trust breaks down, every silence feels suspicious. Every mistake feels intentional. Every missed detail becomes proof of a deeper problem. But when trust is alive, even pain can turn into connection.

If you can’t tell whether it was an elbow or a punch, assume it was an elbow until proven otherwise. That mindset alone has saved countless marriages.

It’s not about ignoring pain; it’s about interpreting it with compassion. We can’t control whether we’ll bump into each other—but we can control how quickly we forgive when we do.

A Quick Tool to Avoid Unintentional Miscommunication

Even the healthiest marriages can get tangled in miscommunication. It’s not usually because one person wants to argue—it’s because two people are trying to love each other in different languages. The good news is that many of those “we just don’t get each other” moments can be prevented with a little intentional clarity.

In my marriage, we discovered this the hard way. I would listen when my wife spoke, nodding as if I understood—but she’d later tell me she didn’t feel heard. Other times, she’d come to me with a problem, and I’d instantly go into “fix-it” mode. That usually made things worse. I thought I was being helpful; she felt I was being dismissive. Our intentions were right, but our methods didn’t match.

So, we created a simple way to communicate what kind of help was needed before the conversation even began. It sounds silly, but it’s been one of the most effective tools we’ve ever used.

When my wife walks into the room and says she needs a few minutes of my time, she’ll sometimes hold up one, two, or three fingers. It’s quick, clear, and it instantly sets the tone for how I should listen.

One finger means: “Just listen.” No problem-solving. No advice. No counterpoints. Just silence, attention, and empathy.

Two fingers means: “I want your thoughts.” This is a conversation, not a counseling session. She’s open to dialogue, but she still wants connection over correction.

Three fingers means: “Fix it.” This is my cue that she’s ready for a solution. She wants a plan, not just understanding.

It’s deceptively simple, but it works because it eliminates guessing. I don’t have to decode tone or body language. I already know what she needs, which helps me show up the right way.

We’ve all been that couple who needed a “1” but got a “3.” Someone just wanted to vent, and instead they got advice. Or one person wanted a fix, and instead they got a long talk about feelings. The intent was loving, but the delivery missed.

This little “finger system” has helped us—and the couples I work with—align our intentions with our actions. It’s a fast way to make sure your good intentions are actually felt as good intentions.

Because here’s the truth: in marriage, most arguments aren’t about big betrayals. They’re about mismatched expectations and misread needs.

You can’t avoid every misunderstanding, but you can reduce most of them with small habits that communicate, “I want to get this right for you.”

And sometimes, that tiny gesture—the raised hand, the one-finger signal—is more powerful than a thousand words.

The Steamroller and the Turtle: Learning to Fight Fair

Misunderstood intent doesn’t just show up in what we say; it shows up in how we fight. Every couple has a rhythm when tension rises—some go loud, some go quiet. For my wife and me, those rhythms couldn’t have been more opposite.

Early in our marriage, things were—let’s just say—rough. Not because we didn’t love each other or lacked commitment, but because we hadn’t yet learned how to “fight fair.” In our tiny, overpriced apartment, we loved deeply, but sometimes we weren’t each other’s biggest fans.

We even had a saying for those moments: “I love you always, but I don’t like you right now.”

That line came from my wife the first time I managed to push her buttons hard enough to need a verbal safety net. Looking back, it was honest and helpful. We loved each other—but we didn’t always like each other’s methods.

You see, I’m a fighter by nature—what I now call the Steamroller. My wife, on the other hand, is a Turtle—she withdraws, retreats into her shell, and needs space to think. As you might imagine, that combination made for some interesting early marriage dynamics.

I grew up in a loud family. We argued, debated, pointed fingers, and expressed love and frustration at high volume. That was normal for us. My wife’s family was the complete opposite. They avoided confrontation like the plague. They got very good at sweeping things under the rug and pretending problems didn’t exist. Before her father passed, he once told me he had eaten spaghetti every Wednesday night for years—not because he loved it, but because he never had the heart to tell his wife he hated it.

That story stuck with me. It was a perfect example of what we in psychology call environmental disposition—learned behavior shaped by the environment we grow up in. My wife and I brought two completely different communication “languages” into our marriage, and those differences became the foundation for conflict.

When we argued, it was predictable: the harder I pushed (Steamroller), the further she pulled away (Turtle). And the further she withdrew, the louder I got. It was a pattern—and one that made “for better or for worse” feel more like for worse and for worse.

Eventually, we both grew tired of replaying the same script. One evening, we decided to stop trying to win and start trying to understand. We sat down, set our defenses aside, and talked. I asked her, “What do I do that makes you feel like you can’t stay in the room during a disagreement?”

She asked me, “What do I do that makes you feel like you have to raise your voice to be heard?”

Those were not easy questions, but they led to honest answers. I learned that my intensity—my tone, my body language, even my quick pace—made her feel cornered. She learned that her silence, which she thought was keeping peace, made me feel shut out. We realized that neither of us was wrong; we were just different.

So, we made a plan. I agreed to slow down and soften my approach, and she agreed to stay present a little longer, even when it felt uncomfortable. Over time, those small adjustments changed everything. We stopped reacting and started relating.

Learning how to conflict well doesn’t mean avoiding it—it means learning how to fight with purpose. Once we stopped trying to “win,” we started to grow. Now, when tension rises, we both recognize the pattern before it spirals. It doesn’t mean every disagreement is easy, but it does mean we can have them without losing connection.

If you take nothing else from our story, take this: it’s possible to turn bad days into better ones—not by avoiding conflict, but by handling it with compassion and curiosity.

Wants vs. Don’t Wants

Every couple wants to be happy, connected, and understood. But I’ve learned that the difference between couples who thrive and those who simply survive often comes down to what motivates them. The question isn’t whether they want a better marriage—it’s why they want it.

In therapy, I often ask couples: “What’s driving your effort right now—what you want, or what you’re trying to avoid?” That question usually stops people in their tracks.

See, there are two main motivators behind nearly every action we take in relationships: wants and don’t wants.

A want is fueled by desire: “I want to love better,” “I want to grow closer,” “I want to understand you.” A don’t want is fueled by avoidance: “I don’t want to argue,” “I don’t want to be hurt again,” “I don’t want to end up like my parents.”

Both can drive change—but only one of them builds something lasting.

When a couple is motivated by don’t wants, they’re always on defense. Their energy is spent avoiding mistakes, sidestepping conflict, or staying quiet to keep the peace. It looks like control, but it’s really fear, insecurity, and avoidance wearing a polite disguise. Over time, that kind of marriage starts to feel small and suffocating. You can’t build something new if all your energy is focused on not breaking what you already have.

When a couple is motivated by wants, the focus shifts. They’re not running from something—they’re moving toward something. They start seeing each disagreement as an opportunity to practice love better. They take risks again. They show vulnerability because they want connection more than they fear rejection.

That’s the quiet difference between a reactive marriage and a responsive one.

The Defensive Marriage

We’ve all seen the defensive version of this play out in real life. One spouse makes a mistake, and the other says, “I’m just not going to bring things up anymore. It’s not worth the fight.” On the surface, that sounds like peace. But it’s really avoidance.

Peace doesn’t mean silence. Peace means safety—safety to speak, safety to disagree, safety to be yourself without walking on eggshells.

I tell couples often, “You can’t make your marriage better by protecting it from truth.” Truth said in love heals faster than silence driven by fear.

So when I ask, “What do you want from this relationship?” I’m not just asking for goals. I’m asking what kind of posture you want to live in. Do you want a marriage that’s reactive and fearful? Or one that’s rooted in hope and intentional growth?

Here’s the beautiful part: you can change from a “don’t want” marriage to a “want” marriage almost instantly. It starts with one simple mental shift.

Instead of saying, “I don’t want to fight,” say, “I want to understand you.” Instead of saying, “I don’t want to mess this up,” say, “I want to show up differently this time.”

Wants move you forward. Don’t wants keep you stuck.

That’s the essence of emotional maturity in marriage—learning to lead your actions with desire instead of defense. It’s a mindset that changes how you show up for your spouse, how you handle stress, and how you interpret the “worse” moments.

Because here’s the truth: every couple will face seasons of worse. But those seasons don’t have to define the marriage; they can refine it. When you know what you’re moving toward, even the hard days feel purposeful.

Grace, Growth, and Choosing Love Anyway

The “for worse” moments are where grace does its best work. When the easy parts of marriage fade, grace steps in to remind us that love was never about convenience. It’s about choosing to see your spouse as worth it, even when you’re tired, frustrated, or hurt.

Grace doesn’t mean you ignore pain. It means you give your spouse a safe place to grow while they’re learning to love you better.

It’s saying, “I see your effort, not just your errors.” It’s reminding yourself, “They’re not my enemy; they’re my partner.”

Growth in marriage is rarely loud or dramatic. It happens in the quiet daily choices—the small apologies, the deeper listening, the second chances you keep giving each other because you’ve decided the marriage itself is worth fighting for.

Love isn’t proven in the better seasons. It’s proven in the worse ones. And when both partners can say, “I’m not here because it’s easy—I’m here because it’s right,” that’s where contentment begins.

Everyday “I Do” Action

Pause before reacting. When something hurts, ask yourself first: “Was that an elbow or a punch?”

Use clarity cues. Before a conversation, communicate what you need—listening, feedback, or action. It’s amazing how much peace that brings.

Flip your language. Replace one “don’t want” statement with a “want” statement this week and notice how it changes your connection.

Give grace first. Grace doesn’t mean weakness—it means remembering that the relationship matters more than winning the moment.

Chapter Summary

“For Better or For Worse” isn’t just a line in a ceremony—it’s the daily reality of a lasting marriage.

The better days remind us why we fell in love. The worse days remind us why commitment matters.

Love doesn’t mean never hurting each other. It means trusting the intention behind the hurt. It means slowing down to ask what your spouse needs, instead of assuming you already know.

When you shift from reacting to connecting, from fearing to wanting, from avoiding to choosing—you discover that the vows you once spoke aren’t history; they’re habits.

And when you live them daily, “I do” stops being a memory and becomes a mindset.
